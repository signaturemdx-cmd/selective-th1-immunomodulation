Disease brief 01 — Autoimmune liver disease

01 — Executive summary

The issue is not whether treatment works. It is how it works.

AIH responds to treatment. The problem is that patients depend on broad immunosuppression for decades, and that diagnosis is frequently delayed.

01

Rare, but easy to miss

The immune system targets hepatocytes and sustains chronic inflammation. Untreated, it can progress to cirrhosis and liver failure.

02

Effective, at a high cost

Appropriate immunosuppression transforms long-term survival — yet it still relies on decades-old, broad-acting drugs.

03

Often advanced at diagnosis

Around 30% already have cirrhosis when diagnosed. Early detection and maintenance therapy are the decisive variables.

04

Targeted therapy is still early evidence

Early B-cell and immunoproteasome programmes are reporting signals, but none replaces standard care yet.

05

The market depends on the definition

AIH-specific drug revenue and broad diagnosis-plus-treatment spend must be read separately for a sound investment view.

02 — Disease

How chronic inflammation rebuilds the liver

AIH is a chronic autoimmune liver disease in which the immune system attacks the body's own hepatocytes. Repeated hepatitis drives fibrosis, then cirrhosis and loss of liver function.

STEP 1
Healthy liverNormal hepatocytes, balanced immunity
STEP 2
Interface hepatitisPeriportal inflammation and hepatocyte injury
STEP 3
FibrosisScar tissue accumulates with repeated injury
STEP 4
CirrhosisArchitectural distortion, declining function
STEP 5
Decompensation · transplantLiver failure, complications, transplant assessment

Not every patient progresses at the same rate. Course varies widely with treatment response, comorbidity and timing of diagnosis.

Type 1 vs Type 2 — separated by serology and phenotype

Two clinically important points

  • Symptoms can be non-specific — fatigue, jaundice, abdominal discomfort — so distinguishing AIH from other liver disease matters.
  • Diagnosis combines autoantibodies, raised IgG, histology and exclusion of viral or toxic causes.

05 — Current treatment

Old drugs, broad suppression, long exposure

Standard care still depends on decades-old broad immunosuppressants. They work for many patients, but adverse effects and relapse management are a lifelong burden.

1ST LINE
Steroid + azathioprinePrednisone or prednisolone plus azathioprine; budesonide plus azathioprine is an alternative in non-cirrhotic disease.
2ND LINE
MMF · tacrolimus · cyclosporineAlternatives for intolerance or an inadequate response to first line.
SALVAGE
Rituximab · belimumabReported salvage options in refractory patients; the evidence base is limited.
LAST RESORT
Liver transplantationFor liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis. Recurrence still requires surveillance after transplant.

Adverse effects by agent

AgentTypical burden
CorticosteroidsDiabetes, osteoporosis, hypertension, cataract, weight gain, infection, mood change

Steroid burden depends on the study. One cited series reports about 80% experiencing adverse effects by two years; another cohort reports up to about 30% on low-dose maintenance. Read this as a range across studies, not a single number.
The gap in standard care. No AIH-specific targeted therapy is established as standard. Care relies heavily on decades-old broad immunosuppression and off-label use.

08 — Investor view

Five reasons, five risks

This section is not investment advice. It is a frame for weighing development stage, market definition and the uncertainty of the evidence.

Why it draws attention

  • A clear unmet need: a therapy that reduces steroid burden
  • A maintenance structure: remission upkeep and relapse control
  • Orphan incentives: exclusivity, tax and fees
  • More precise mechanisms: B-cell and immunoproteasome approaches
  • Adjacent expansion: PBC, PSC and other autoimmune liver disease

What to weigh against it

  • A small patient population limits recruitment and commercial scale
  • Heterogeneity across type, serology and stage
  • Difficult endpoints: definitions of relapse and biochemical remission matter
  • Small early trials: tiny n, little confirmatory evidence
  • Price and access: orphan pricing and reimbursement remain variables
Diligence questions: Which population? Which endpoint? Is the control appropriate? Was steroid tapering assessed alongside?
