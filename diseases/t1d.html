Disease brief 02 — Endocrine autoimmunity

Insulin keeps you alive.
It does not stop the disease.

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system destroys pancreatic β-cells. Insulin replacement is a means of survival, not a cure.

01 — Executive summary

Insulin sustains life. It does not change the cause.

Glucose technology has advanced sharply. Autoimmune destruction has not stopped. Development is shifting from control toward immune intervention and cell therapy.

01

T1D is an autoimmune disease

Immune destruction of β-cells produces absolute insulin deficiency and requires lifelong management.

02

Onset is not sudden

Immune signs precede symptoms. Early detection is where diabetic ketoacidosis is prevented.

03

The ceiling of current care is clear

Insulin, CGM and automated delivery improve glucose; none halts autoimmunity.

02 — Disease

Three stages: antibodies come before symptoms

T1D is not fundamentally a glucose disorder: the immune system removes the cells that make insulin. Staging by autoantibody count and dysglycaemia defines a window before clinical onset.

STAGE 1
Two or more autoantibodiesNormal glucose · no symptoms
STAGE 2
Two or more autoantibodies · dysglycaemiaAbnormal glucose · still asymptomatic
STAGE 3
Clinical onsetSymptoms · insulin required

Core autoantibodies

  • Insulin — autoantibody to insulin
  • GAD — glutamic acid decarboxylase
  • IA-2 — islet antigen-2
  • ZnT8 — zinc transporter 8
Most patients have no family history. Screening studies report asymptomatic staged disease in roughly 0.3% of the population.

Staging combines autoantibody count with glucose measures such as an oral glucose tolerance test. A single positive antibody does not define disease.

08 — Investor view

Five reasons, five risks

Not optimism — verifiable logic. Each reason carries a counter-question that must be answered.

Why it matters

  • Clear biology: β-cell destruction is an unambiguous target
  • An early-stage market: stages 1 and 2 are now intervenable
  • A combined stack: devices, drugs and cell therapy
  • Unmet need: targets are missed and acute risk persists
  • Pipeline depth across immune, JAK and cell approaches

What to weigh

  • Immune intervention safety: infection and long-term immune effects
  • Cell therapy manufacturing: donor, quality and cost
  • Dependence on immunosuppression limits the value of a cure
  • Commercial diffusion: what teplizumab has shown
  • Market scope: the estimate swings with the definition
Diligence questions: At which stage was efficacy shown? Is C-peptide preserved? Can it work without immunosuppression? Does it hold in children and adults alike?
